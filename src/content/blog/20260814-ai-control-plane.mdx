On 6 August 2026, Unity AI Gateway became generally available on Databricks. The idea of an AI
control pane is not a novel one, and this is part of a broader emerging trend in how
enterprises are expected to work with AI in the future. AWS's AgentCore became generally available
on October 2025, while Azure's dedicated AI Gateway tier entered general availability on July 2026.
Increasingly, enterprises are beginning to realise that what drives value in the AI space is not always
about finding the most powerful or revolutionary product. Rather, setting the right control framework
and infrastructure in place is what keep things ticking in the long term.

So, what exactly are the problems that enterprise AI teams face which makes a control pane that much more enticing?
The clue lies in the core offerings behind Databricks's Unity AI Gateway.

## 1. Visibility over what's running and who can use it
It is important to understand that AI Gateway is not an independent layer within the Databricks ecosystem. 
Databricks describes the Gateway as a single, centralized governance layer for an AI estate, built on Unity Catalog.

At first glance, having a centralized catalog can look like little more than an organizational convenience. 
Its real value, however, is governance. By establishing a governed inventory of the AI services, agents, 
and tools available to the organization, AI teams gain visibility into what capabilities are being exposed. 
They can then centrally control who can access them and how they are used.

That visibility matters as without it, an employee could inadvertently use tools that pose a risk to the organization.
Take for example an unapproved malicious agent that could connect to sensitive enterprise data. All it takes is for 
an unknowing employee to approve it - and without even realizing, sensitive data is sent to external services resulting
in a serious compliance risk event. Even if the agent was introduced with good intentions, we can see how it can be dangerous
to leave the governing decisions to independent employees. Rather, it makes monumentally more sense to leave the decision making 
to teams whose expertise lies specifically in this area. The AI Gateway supports this by giving organizations a location to 
understand what tools exists, who is using it, what it can access, and what controls are applied on it. 

Because AI Gateway is built on Unity  Catalog, organizations can extend an existing governance model to AI rather 
than creating and maintaining a parallel governance framework. This can be a real difference-maker for organizations 
that have been actively increasing AI fluency, allowing solutions to move from experimentation into transparent, governed production.

An effective control pane is hence one that first and foremost, provides transparency in a manner that does not require a governance silo. 

## 2. Keeping AI spend from spiralling
When third-party AI services began to gain traction, organizations were excited by the prospect of significant cost savings brought 
about by increased productivity. Fast forward to today—that potential very much exists, but organizations are beginning to understand 
that realizing those savings is less straightforward than initially expected.

To understand where AI spend can come from, we must first understand the charging models. AI services can be paid for using a 
pay-as-you-go model, where charges are based on actual consumption. They can also be offered through subscription models, where 
organizations pay a fixed fee for a defined level of access or usage. In many cases, pricing combines elements of both. Left unchecked, 
organizations run the risk of either generating unexpectedly high usage costs or consuming their allocated capacity far faster than intended.

These are two very real economic problems that AI Gateway aims to address. The first is budget control, which governs how much can be spent. 
The second is rate limiting, which governs how quickly capacity can be consumed. Used together, they provide much tighter control over how AI 
services are consumed.

AI Gateway takes this control a step further by allowing limits to be applied not only to individual users, but also to service principals 
and user groups. This shifts the conversation from “How much should this user be allowed to consume?” to “How much AI consumption is appropriate 
for this team or workload?”. Additionally, alerts can be configured to provide visibility as thresholds are approached, while enforcement mechanisms 
can be arranged to stop consumption from continuing beyond customized limits. This makes sense, as controllers should not have to manually monitor 
AI consumption and intervene whenever usage approaches a limit.

Ultimately, an effective AI control plane should be able to define, track, and enforce AI consumption in a way that reflects how organizations 
are actually structured.

## 3. Controlling what AI is actually allowed to do and respond
The past segments have largely addressed issues about how we administer AI, but regulating what AI produces and what it is allowed to do is of critical 
importance as well. This becomes increasingly important as more powerful agents are given permission to act on behalf of users. Such use cases already 
exist, with common examples including agents that can execute code or send emails autonomously.

Now, imagine that an agent executes code that corrupts a repository, or sends an email containing potentially discriminatory content or 
Personally Identifiable Information (PII). The agent may have been authorized to perform the action, but that does not necessarily mean the action itself 
was appropriate.Needless to say, guardrails must remain in place even as organizations continue to pursue the productivity gains offered by autonomous agent.

AI Gateway provides a centralized location for applying these controls. Databricks provides built-in guardrails for common risks such as unsafe content, 
PII, and prompt injection, while also allowing organizations to create custom service policies for requirements specific to their environment. The key 
learning point here is that generic guardrails are important as a means of safeguarding against common pitfalls, but a true control plane should not force 
organizations into a fixed set of controls. It should also allow these guardrails to be adapted to the organization’s specific operational, technical, and 
security needs when the built-in options are deemed insufficient.


## So does your organisation actually need one?
Well, not really. It is important to treat the control plane just like any product, and evaluate it carefully. 
Listed below are a (non-comprehensive) set of considerations:

1.  Integration with the existing technology stack: A control plane is most valuable when it fits naturally into the organization's existing governance systems, 
    as well as its downstream applications. First, this helps prevent governance silos and allows security and access policies to be maintained consistently. 
    Second, it ensures that applications do not require substantial redesign simply because their AI services are incompatible with the control plane.

2.  Cost relative to the scale of the AI estate: A control plane becomes increasingly useful as both AI consumption and the underlying ecosystem expand. 
    Increased consumption can mean usage spanning multiple teams, business units, and applications. A more complex ecosystem may also bring together models, agents, 
    and MCP servers from various providers. Having a common governance layer can help reduce this fragmentation, particularly compared with an environment where 
    everything is owned and managed by a small team. For a small AI estate, the additional cost of a dedicated control plane may be difficult to justify.

3.  Risk profile of the AI workloads: A control plane ultimately aims to reduce organizational risk. The greater the potential risk associated with AI workloads, 
    the stronger the case for implementing a control plane to provide tighter controls and guardrails. On the other hand, it may be overkill if the organization's 
    primary AI use case is, for example, internal summarization.

4.  Operational overhead: Purchasing a control plane alone does not guarantee effective governance. It is a tool after all, and someone must have the right skill 
    set to operate it - from defining policies to managing budgets and monitoring alerts. If the organization does not have the right personnel to operate the control 
    plane properly, it risks becoming a white elephant.

Even if an organization is unable to justify a control plane at its current stage of AI adoption, it should still form part of the longer-term roadmap if the intention 
is to scale AI usage over time.

The increasing acceptance of a control plane as a core component of an organization's AI infrastructure shows that organizations are becoming more deliberate about how they use and govern AI. 
However, despite being a relatively new concept, new capabilities are already being introduced at a rapid pace. Organizations that understand this early and prioritizes adoption will likely
find themselves in a much stronger position as  their AI adoption continues to grow.
